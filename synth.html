Sound synthesis

An oscillator makes a wave, a filter shapes its tone, envelopes shape it over time and an LFO adds movement. Play the synth, and follow the guide to learn what each part does.

What sound is

Sound is air pressure moving back and forth very quickly. A speaker cone pushes the air, pulls it back, pushes again, and your ear picks up the wobble. The oscilloscope at the top of the synth draws that wobble: time runs left to right, and the line goes up when the air is pushed and down when it's pulled.

The wave repeats the same shape over and over. How many times it repeats in one second is its frequency, measured in hertz (Hz), and that's what you hear as pitch. The A above middle C repeats 440 times a second, so it's 440 Hz. Go up an octave and the frequency doubles: the next A is 880 Hz. Play a low note and a high note and watch the waves squeeze together.

How tall the wave is, its amplitude, is how loud it sounds. Its shape is the third thing, and it decides the tone, or timbre. That's what the next lesson is about.

    Oscillators and waveforms

    The oscillator is the part of a synth that makes the raw wave. Classic synths offer four shapes, picked here with the Wave buttons above the knobs:

    • Sine: smooth and pure, like a whistle or a flute. It's the simplest sound there is.
    • Triangle: still soft, a little brighter.
    • Saw: bright and buzzy, the starting point for strings, brass and most dance leads and basses.
    • Square: hollow and woody, like a clarinet or an old video game.

    Why do they sound different at the same pitch? Every wave except the sine is really a stack of sine waves: the main one, called the fundamental, plus quieter ones at 2, 3, 4… times its frequency, called harmonics. The spectrum view under the oscilloscope shows them as peaks. A sine has one peak. A saw has every harmonic, so it looks like a staircase going down to the right. A square has only the odd ones.

    F3 Detune adds a second oscillator, slightly out of tune with the first. The two drift in and out of step, which you hear as a slow shimmer that makes the sound wider and thicker.

      The filter

      Most synths work by subtractive synthesis: start with a wave full of harmonics, like a saw, then carve away what you don't want. The tool for carving is the filter.

      This synth has a low-pass filter: it lets low frequencies pass and turns down the high ones. K2 Cutoff sets where that starts. Turn it down and the sound gets darker and more muffled, like music heard through a wall. Turn it up and it opens back up. In the spectrum view, the green line is the filter: harmonics under its flat part pass, and where the line drops, they get quieter.

      K1 Filter type changes what the filter keeps. Low-pass is the classic one, and the one this lesson uses. High-pass does the opposite: it removes the lows and leaves a thin, airy sound. Band-pass keeps only a band around the cutoff, like a voice on the phone. Notch cuts out just that band and keeps the rest. Watch the green line change shape with each one.

      K3 Resonance boosts the frequencies right at the cutoff, so a bump appears on the green line. A little adds bite. A lot makes the filter whistle, and sweeping the cutoff then gives the classic "wow" sound of acid and dance music.

        Envelopes: ADSR

        Real sounds change over time. A piano note starts with a hit and fades away. A violin can swell in slowly. An organ stays at the same volume until you let go. The envelope gives a synth note that shape, in four stages:

        • K5 Attack: how long the note takes to reach full volume after you press the key.
        • K6 Decay: how long it then takes to fall to the sustain level.
        • K7 Sustain: the level the note holds while you keep the key down. It's a level, not a time.
        • K8 Release: how long it takes to fade out after you let go.

        The envelope graph in the synth panel draws the shape, and a dot follows your note through the stages as it plays.

        Try it

          5

          The filter envelope

          Acoustic instruments are brightest right when a note starts, then get darker as it fades. A synth copies that by letting the envelope move the filter too. K4 Env amount sets how far the filter opens on each note, up to four octaves above the cutoff. In this synth the filter follows the same ADSR as the volume.

          Set the cutoff low, turn up the env amount and every note starts bright and closes down: the "pluck" and "squelch" behind countless basslines. Watch the green filter line jump up and slide back down in the spectrum view as you play.

            LFO: movement

            An LFO, a low-frequency oscillator, is a wave too slow to hear, under about 20 times a second. Instead of making sound, it moves another control up and down for you. F1 LFO rate sets how fast, F2 LFO depth how far. What it moves changes the effect completely:

            • Filter: the tone opens and closes, from a slow sweep to the dubstep "wobble".
            • Pitch: the note bends slightly up and down. That's vibrato, like a singer's.
            • Volume: the sound pulses louder and quieter. That's tremolo.

            Choose the target with the "LFO to" buttons in the synth panel. On the MiniLab 3, the mod strip adds a separate vibrato as you slide it up, the way players add expression live.
