1

Notes and steps

Western music uses just 12 notes, and they repeat up and down the keyboard. The white keys are named with the letters A to G. Each black key sits between two white keys and takes its name from them: the key just above C is C♯ (C sharp), which is the same key as D♭ (D flat).

The smallest distance between two notes is a half step (also called a semitone): from one key to the very next, black or white. Two half steps make a whole step. Twelve half steps bring you to the same letter again, one octave higher. It sounds like the same note, just brighter.

The distance between any two notes is called an interval. Intervals are the building blocks of everything that follows, so try it: play one note, then another, and see how far apart they are.

2

Scales

A scale is a set of seven notes picked out of the twelve, played in order. Which seven you pick follows a fixed pattern of whole steps (W) and half steps (H).

The major scale follows W W H W W W H. It sounds bright and settled. Start it on C and you get only white keys: C D E F G A B C. The natural minor scale follows W H W W H W W and sounds darker. Start the same pattern on any other key and black keys appear, because the pattern stays the same.

Each note of a scale has a number called its degree. The first note, 1, is the root or home note. The scale gives a song its key: a song "in C major" mostly uses the notes of the C major scale.

3

Chords

A chord is three or more notes played together. The most common chord is the triad: take a scale, play degree 1, skip one, play degree 3, skip one, play degree 5. That is stacking intervals called thirds.

Two kinds of third make the difference between the two main chords. A major third is 4 half steps, a minor third is 3.

A major chord is a major third with a minor third on top (4 + 3). It sounds bright. A minor chord swaps them (3 + 4) and sounds darker. Only the middle note moves, by a single half step.

The notes of a chord can be stacked in any order. These different orders are called inversions: C E G, E G C and G C E are all a C major chord. That's why the game accepts any inversion and any octave.

4

Harmony

Harmony is how notes sound together, and how chords move from one to the next. It starts with the key. Build a triad on every degree of a major scale using only notes from that scale, and you get seven chords that belong to the key.

Musicians number them with Roman numerals: uppercase for major chords (I, IV, V), lowercase for minor (ii, iii, vi), and ° for the one diminished chord (vii°). Every major key has the same pattern, so a progression written as numerals works in any key.

Each chord has a job. Tonic chords (I, and also iii and vi) feel like home. Subdominant chords (IV, ii) step away from home. Dominant chords (V, vii°) build tension that wants to resolve back to I. That pull from V to I is the engine behind most songs you know.
